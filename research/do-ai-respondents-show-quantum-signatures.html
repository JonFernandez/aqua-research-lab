Do AI respondents show human-like quantum signatures?

QQ-equality and disjunction tests of LLM-simulated respondents against historical human benchmarks.

  • Crystelle Jel Soriano · CERNEN
  • Proceso Fernandez, Jr. · CERNEN; Ateneo de Manila University

In brief

Language models are being proposed as stand-ins for people in surveys. Human judgments show two well-documented patterns that quantum-probability models capture: question-order effects that obey a parameter-free rule called the QQ equality, and the "disjunction effect", where not knowing an outcome lowers choices below both known cases. We asked whether AI respondents reproduce that structure, not just similar percentages.

The question

When language models simulate survey respondents, do they reproduce the structure of human judgments, rather than merely similar answer percentages?

Why it matters

Simulated respondents, sometimes called "silicon samples", are being proposed as stand-ins for people (Argyle et al., 2023). Showing a human-like effect is not the same as having human-like structure. The closest prior work tested the QQ equality on one open model via next-token probabilities and found its answers too deterministic to judge (Kang, 2026, preprint). This study sampled many personas instead.

Central finding

Mixed, and the evidence is thin

The two model/setting combinations behaved differently. Matching human percentages is not enough: test the structure of judgments before AI respondents stand in for people.

15,600model responses with sampled personas
2model/setting combinations, one run each
1informative question-order (QQ) test
  • Both combinations showed question-order effects.
  • On the one informative QQ test, one combination broke the QQ equality; the other showed no detected violation.
  • Only one combination showed a disjunction effect, on the textbook gamble, and it did not carry over to a committee scenario with the same payoffs.

Evidence type: language-model outputs compared with published historical human results. No new human data.

Results in numbers

Model names are given exactly as reported on the poster: API aliases gemini-2.5-flash (thinking off) and gemini-3.8-flash (reasoning on), run 8–9 November 2026 at temperature 1. They should not be read as describing other versions or current products.

  • QQ test (Clinton–Gore). 2.5 Flash broke the equality: q = −0.18, 95% CI excludes 0 (historical human |q| = 0.003). 3.8 Flash showed no detected violation: q = −0.04, 95% CI −0.10 to 0.02; post hoc equivalence was supported at a ±0.09 margin (90% CI −0.087 to 0.007). Equivalence to the human value was not tested.
  • Control (Rose–Jackson, background text before each question). 3.8 Flash's q excluded 0 but was small and positive (+0.03), whereas the human deviation was negative (|q| = 0.15).
  • Disjunction. 3.8 Flash chose to play (or approve) after a win / loss / unresolved outcome at 56% / 48% / 14% on the textbook gamble (D = 0.34; historical human benchmark 69% / 59% / 36%, D = 0.23), but 99% / 20% / 19% in the same-payoff committee version.
  • Post hoc. All four QQ violations (one saturated) survive Bonferroni correction over six tests (max adjusted p = .002).

Approach

Two experiments with sampled personas. One asked historical Gallup question pairs in both orders to test the QQ equality. The other posed a classic gamble, and a committee version with the same payoffs, after a win, a loss or an unresolved outcome. Predictions were specified in advance.

How the study worked
  • Experiment 1, question order. Three Gallup question pairs (Moore, 2002), each asked in both orders, with 500 personas ("US adult, late 1990s") per order per model; the second question was asked in the same chat. Clinton–Gore and White–Black were asked back to back. White–Black turned out saturated, so Clinton–Gore was the one informative test. Rose–Jackson, with background text before each question, was the control, where the QQ equality is not expected to hold.
  • Experiment 2, what-if choices. The Tversky–Shafir gamble (50% win $200 / 50% lose $100) and a committee version (approving a second round after a pilot result that was strong, weak or not yet known), with 300 personas per condition per model in separate chats.
  • Inference. Targets each model's answer distribution under the sampled personas and prompts, not a human population. Persona-paired standard errors were also checked.

Limitations

  • This does not validate AI respondents as substitutes for people.
  • Two models from one provider, one run each; model generation and reasoning setting are not separated.
  • The human comparisons are historical benchmarks with reconstructed wording.
  • The textbook result may reflect recall of the published study, which is an untested explanation.
  • These signatures do not uniquely identify a quantum mechanism, and nothing here claims a language model is physically quantum.
More limitations
  • Personas are not people, and the "late 1990s" anchor lets models use hindsight and recalled poll figures.
  • Yes/No answer order was not counterbalanced.
  • The QQ conclusion rests on one question pair.
  • A large human replication did not reproduce the original gamble effect (Ziano et al., 2021).

Next research question

Does the structure hold when the textbook gamble is disguised, when the equivalence margin is fixed in advance, when the same model runs with reasoning on and off across repeat runs, and when a matched human sample answers the same wording? A protocol for the disguised gamble has been written.
