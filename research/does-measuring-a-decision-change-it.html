Does measuring a decision change it?

A quantum-probability design check for AI advice in university selection committees.

  • Proceso Fernandez, Jr. · Ateneo de Manila University; CERNEN
  • Crystelle Jel Soriano · CERNEN

In brief

AI scores are being tested in selective admissions review. When a committee sees the AI's view, and whether members first write down their own judgment, are design choices. Quantum-probability models predict that recording a decision can itself change later judgments, much as a measurement can. Before running any human study, we simulated the planned experiment to check whether it could tell that account apart from classical ones.

The question

Could recording a provisional judgment change how a committee responds to later AI advice, and could a planned experiment distinguish competing explanations?

Why it matters

Algorithmic scores are being tested in selective admissions review (Lee et al., 2026). In studies of individuals, recording one's own answer first was linked to less agreement with the AI, most clearly for misleading advice (Fogliato et al., 2022; Yan, 2026), but those studies bundle recording with timing.

Within the authors' literature search, no study had varied the timing of AI recommendations within committee deliberation, or compared a quantum group model with classical rivals on held-out human data. Grant panels, hiring boards and procurement committees face the same timing choice.

Central finding

Under the tested assumptions

66 simulated committees could tell the quantum-probability model apart from the tested classical models, but could not measure each effect precisely.

91–100%of 200 synthetic datasets in which held-out comparison separated the quantum model from the classical ones
±0.10–0.22typical uncertainty for each effect (median 95% CI half-width), against a ±0.05 target
≈ 0.09predicted drop in the committee's shift toward the AI from recording alone, in the quantum model

Evidence type: a simulation of a planned experiment. No human committees took part.

Approach

Simulated committees scored fictional scholarship applications in three conditions: AI advice before discussion; AI advice, then a privately recorded decision; or a recorded decision, then AI advice. Committee members followed either a quantum-probability model or classical belief-adjustment models, and we checked whether the planned design could recover which model generated the data.

How the simulation worked
  • Design. 66 three-person committees; each rates the same 12 fictional scholarship files once, 4 per condition, in a Latin square.
  • Conditions. C1: AI score, then discuss. C2: AI score, record shortlist yes/no, then discuss. C3: record shortlist yes/no, AI score, then discuss. Every condition starts with reading the file and ends with a committee score.
  • Outcome. Shift toward the AI, S = (committee − baseline) ÷ (AI − baseline), where the baseline is the mean no-AI rating of 20 separate raters and the AI score is baseline ± 2.
  • Quantum model. The Trueblood–Busemeyer (2011) model; the code reproduces their worked example. Treating recording as a Lüders projection is the authors' modeling assumption, motivated by Kvam et al. (2015).
  • Classical models. Belief adjustment (Hogarth & Einhorn, 1992): R1, recording changes nothing; R1c, recording damps later updating. In every model, members update toward each other and the committee score is the mean belief.
  • Checks fixed in advance. Prediction pattern; model recovery (fit on 53 committees, test on 13; pass at ≥ 80%); precision (target ±0.05). A balanced-reliance subset was analyzed post hoc.

Code and protocol are available from the authors on request.

Limitations

  • Simulation only. This tests a study design, not human cognition.
  • Noise levels are assumed, not measured.
  • Bayesian and social-influence explanations were not tested, and the recording step was not ablated.
  • We make no claim that brains are quantum systems. The model borrows the mathematics of quantum theory, not the physics.
More detail on what fell short
  • Exact recovery of the commitment model (R1c) fell short of the planned ≥ 80%, at 72% and 53% in the two comparisons. In a post hoc balanced-reliance subset, separation was 98–100% (n = 45/51).
  • The quantum model's recording effect was detected in 33% of datasets generated by that model.
  • Detecting the 0.09 effect with 80% power would need the C2 − C1 half-width to fall from 0.19 to about 0.063: roughly nine times more committees (approximate, assuming unchanged variance), or an individual-level study.

Next research question

How noisy are real committees? The next steps are to pilot committees to measure noise, add Bayesian and social-influence models as rivals, and test the recording effect with individuals first.
